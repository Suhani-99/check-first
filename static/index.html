It will never tell you a message is genuine. Nothing can reliably do that, and a wrong reassurance is what costs families their savings. Why we built it that way →

Whatever you paste, you get the same three things.

  1. The pressure it can seeUrgency, secrecy, a payment route that doesn't fit, someone claiming to be an official — quoted back to you in their own words.
  2. What that means, plainlyNo scores, no jargon. Two or three sentences anyone can follow while their hands are shaking.
  3. A way to find out for yourselfSomething you can do right now — and what to do if it doesn't settle it, down to if the money has already gone.

A voice can be copied from a few seconds of a public video. A shared secret cannot. A safe word is one word your family agrees in advance, and asks for whenever someone rings in a panic asking for money.

1
Pick a word nobody could guess Not a pet, a child, a street or a birthday — those are all on social media. Something unrelated and vivid: brass kettle, yellow scooter.
2
Agree it out loud, in person Never over WhatsApp, SMS or email. A hacked account would hand it straight to a scammer.
3
Keep the circle small Immediate family only — everyone who might be asked for money, and everyone who might be impersonated.
4
Use it once, for practice Ring each other and ask for it, so it feels normal. In a real panic, people forget a plan they have never used.
5
Ask for it before any money moves "What's our safe word?" A copied voice can sound exactly like your child. It cannot know a word that was never spoken online.
If they don't know it: send nothing and share no codes, however convincing they sound. Hang up and ring the number already saved in your phone.
Shield does not store your safe word. It is never typed here, never sent anywhere, never saved on this device — it should live only in your family's heads. A safe word a computer holds is a safe word that can leak.
