How we measure.

A safety tool that hides its own failure rate is asking for trust it has not earned. So here are our numbers, including the ones that make us look worse.

Two sets, on purpose

100%
Committed set — 40 cases. Written and locked into version control before any tuning, so the labels could not be adjusted to flatter the result. 20 scams, 20 ordinary messages.
70%
Adversarial set — 20 cases. Written afterwards, specifically to break the system. Real fraud patterns from people who receive these calls, plus deliberate attacks on our own assumptions.

The first number alone would be misleading. Scoring full marks means a test set has stopped teaching you anything — so we built a harder one and published what it did to us.

The six it got wrong

All six failures came from the adversarial set. Every one is instructive, and three of them are not really fixable — they are the honest edge of what reading a single message can do.

  • A surprise giftfalse alarm

    “Don't tell mum, I'm getting her an anniversary present — can you send me 3,000?” Real secrecy, real urgency, real money, and completely innocent. Families keep harmless secrets, and our rules cannot always tell those from the dangerous kind.

  • A legitimate code requestfalse alarm

    A car was sold but still registered in the seller's name, so the buyer genuinely needed a code to renew insurance. We flag every request for a code, deliberately. We would rather be wrong here than teach anyone that sharing one is ever fine.

  • The broken phonefalse alarm — and correct

    “My phone died in the rain, I'm on a shopkeeper's phone, send 10,000 to his account.” Unknown number, urgency, a stranger's payment details — every red flag, and entirely real. We flag it, and we think that is right: the plan then asks for the family safe word, which the real person answers in seconds and a scammer cannot.

  • A courtesy callmissed

    “Just confirming you know your deposit matures on the 14th. No action needed.” The opening move of a longer con, and it asks for nothing at all. Reading one message, there is nothing yet to catch.

  • A neighbour in needmissed

    “Aunty from B-wing here, my son's away, could you help me with 2,000?” Friendly, small, plausible, no urgency and no payment route named.

  • Getting back in touchmissed

    “I know we haven't spoken in years, but I'm in a bad spot. Can we talk?” From a hacked account. No money mentioned — that comes after you reply.

What the failures have in common

Read together they say something uncomfortable and important: our signals are neither necessary nor sufficient. A completely legitimate message can carry every red flag we look for. A real scam can carry none of them. No amount of tuning fixes that, because it is not a tuning problem — it is the limit of judging a single message by its contents.

Which is the argument for the whole design. If detection cannot be made reliable, a product that depends on detection cannot be made safe. Ours does not depend on it. It reads what it can, tells you what it saw, and always hands you a way to find out for yourself.

How these numbers were produced. A script runs every case through the same code the live site uses — not a separate test version — and compares the verdict against labels committed to version control beforehand. The committed set was timestamped before any tuning began, so it cannot have been adjusted to match the results.
